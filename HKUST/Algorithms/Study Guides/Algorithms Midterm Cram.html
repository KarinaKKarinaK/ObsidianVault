Big O: how cost grows

Lecture 1, Tutorial 1, HW1 Q1, and one 5-point table on every midterm.

Analogy Big O is ignoring the price tag and looking at the shape of the curve. If you pay a flat fee plus 3 dollars per item, and I pay 300 dollars per item, you are cheaper for every large order even though your fee is bigger. $O(g)$ means "for big inputs, my cost is at most a constant times $g$". $\Omega$ is "at least". $\Theta$ is "exactly this shape".
The three symbols, in plain words first

We compare how fast two functions grow when $n$ gets huge. Constant factors do not count ($3n$ and $100n$ are "the same"), and small $n$ does not count.

  • $f = O(g)$: $f$ grows no faster than $g$. $g$ is a ceiling. $5n+3$ is $O(n^2)$ because for big $n$, $n^2$ is far above it.
  • $f = \Omega(g)$: $f$ grows at least as fast as $g$. $g$ is a floor. $n^2$ is $\Omega(n)$.
  • $f = \Theta(g)$: same growth rate. Ceiling and floor at once. $3n^2+7n$ is $\Theta(n^2)$.

Analogy: $O$ is "I earn at most about as much as you", $\Omega$ is "at least about as much", $\Theta$ is "about the same". "About" means up to a constant factor, and only once $n$ is large.

Formal versions, write exactly this if asked to define:

  • $f = O(g)$: there exist constants $c > 0$ and $n_0$ with $f(n) \le c\,g(n)$ for all $n \ge n_0$.
  • $f = \Omega(g)$: there exist $c > 0$ and $n_0$ with $f(n) \ge c\,g(n)$ for all $n \ge n_0$.
  • $f = \Theta(g)$: both hold. To prove $\Theta$, prove $O$ and $\Omega$ separately.
  • "Worst-case running time is $\Theta(g)$" means two things: never more than $O(g)$ on any input, and some family of inputs really costs $\Omega(g)$.
Recipe: compare two functions in 20 seconds
  1. Simplify each side: drop constants, keep the dominant term, change every log to $\log_2$ (base never matters).
  2. Use the ladder: constants $\lt$ $\log\log n$ $\lt$ $(\log n)^{c}$ $\lt$ $n^{\epsilon}$ (any $\epsilon \gt 0$) $\lt$ $n \log n$ $\lt$ $n^2$ $\lt$ $n^c$ $\lt$ $2^{n}$ $\lt$ $n!$ $\lt$ $n^n$.
  3. Exponents: constants added to an exponent do nothing ($2^{n+5} = \Theta(2^n)$); constants multiplying an exponent change the class ($2^{2n} \ne O(2^n)$, $5^n$ vs $7^n$: $5^n = O(7^n)$ only).
  4. The swap identity: $a^{\log_b n} = n^{\log_b a}$. So $3^{\log_2 n} = n^{\log_2 3} = n^{1.585}$ and $2^{\log_2 \log_2 n} = \log_2 n$.
  5. Weird pairs: substitute $m = \log n$ (or $m = \sqrt n$) and compare the simpler functions of $m$.
  6. Sums: $\sum_{i=1}^n i^c = \Theta(n^{c+1})$, $\sum 1/i = \Theta(\log n)$, $\sum_{i=1}^n \sqrt{i}\,n = \Theta(n^{2.5})$, $\sum 2^{-i} = \Theta(1)$.
The A / B / U table (S25 Q2, S26 Q1a) "A" if $T_A = o(T_B)$ strictly (A always faster for large $n$), "B" if the reverse, "U" if the given information cannot decide. U appears exactly when one side is only an O or only an $\Omega$ bound, because then the true function could be anything below or above. If both sides are $\Theta$ you can always decide.

HW1 Q1 answers, with the trick for each
  • $n^{\log\log n}$ vs $(\log n)^{\log n}$: take $\log_2$ of both: $\log\log n \cdot \log n$ versus $\log n \cdot \log\log n$. Equal, so $\Theta$.
  • $2^{\sqrt{2\log n}}$ vs $\sqrt n = 2^{\frac12 \log n}$: compare exponents $\sqrt{2 \log n}$ vs $\frac12\log n$; the square root loses. $O$ only.
  • $n!$ vs $n^{n/2}$: $n! \ge (n/2)^{n/2}$ and that already beats $n^{n/2}$ up to a $2^{n/2}$ factor, while $n!$ is much more. $\Omega$ only.
  • $4^{\sqrt n}$ vs $2^n$: exponents $2\sqrt n$ vs $n$. $O$ only.
  • $\sum 1/i = H_n = \Theta(\log n)$ vs $n \log n$: $O$ only.
Series tricks that earn proof points
  • Upper bound a sum: replace every term by the largest term. $\sum_{i=1}^n i \le n \cdot n$.
  • Lower bound a sum (half trick): keep only the top half of the terms, each is at least the middle value. $\sum_{i=1}^n i \ge \frac n2 \cdot \frac n2$.
  • Harmonic sum: group into blocks $\{1\}, \{\frac12,\frac13\}, \{\frac14..\frac17\}, \dots$; each block sums to between $\frac12$ and 1; there are $\log_2 n$ blocks, so $H_n = \Theta(\log n)$.
  • Geometric sum: $\sum_{i=0}^{k} r^i = \frac{r^{k+1}-1}{r-1}$; if $r \lt 1$ it is $\Theta(1)$, if $r \gt 1$ it is $\Theta(r^k)$ (last term dominates), if $r = 1$ it is $k+1$.
  • Triple loop (Tutorial 1 Q6): upper bound $n^3$; lower bound: restrict to $i \le n/4$, $j \ge 3n/4$, then the inner loop runs at least $n/2$ times, giving $\ge \frac{n}{4}\cdot\frac{n}{4}\cdot\frac{n}{2}$.
Why it matters When a product manager asks "will this still work with ten times the users", Big O is the honest answer. An $O(n^2)$ feature that is fine in a demo with 100 rows falls over at 100,000. Knowing the ladder lets you say "this needs a different design" before you write code.
Try it: order these from slowest-growing to fastest: $n \log n$, $2^{\log_2 n}$, $(\log n)^{100}$, $n^{1.01}$, $3^{\log_2 n}$, $n!$
$(\log n)^{100} \lt 2^{\log_2 n} = n \lt n^{1.01} \lt n \log n$? Careful: $n \log n$ vs $n^{1.01}$: $\log n \lt n^{0.01}$ eventually, so $n \log n \lt n^{1.01}$. Order: $(\log n)^{100}$, $n$, $n\log n$, $n^{1.01}$, $3^{\log_2 n} = n^{1.585}$, $n!$.

Recurrences: the cost of recursion

HW1 Q2, Quiz 3, and a 4 to 5 point question on every midterm. Usually "do not use the Master Theorem, show your steps".

Analogy A recurrence is a family tree of chores. The root does some work and hires $a$ children, each with a pile $b$ times smaller. Total cost is the sum over all generations. Three things can happen: the top generation does most of the work (root wins), every generation does the same amount (multiply by the number of generations), or the bottom generation of tiny tasks is so numerous it wins (leaves win).
Simplified Master Theorem (for $T(n) = aT(n/b) + n$)
CaseConditionAnswerWho wins
1$a \gt b$$\Theta(n^{\log_b a})$leaves
2$a = b$$\Theta(n \log n)$every level ties
3$a \lt b$$\Theta(n)$root
General form $T(n) = aT(n/b) + n^d$: compare $a$ with $b^d$. $a \gt b^d$ gives $\Theta(n^{\log_b a})$; $a = b^d$ gives $\Theta(n^d \log n)$; $a \lt b^d$ gives $\Theta(n^d)$. Memorize: number of leaves is $a^{\log_b n} = n^{\log_b a}$.
Recipe A: repeated expansion (what the graders want to see) 
T(n) = a T(n/b) + f(n)
     = a [ a T(n/b²) + f(n/b) ] + f(n)   = a² T(n/b²) + a f(n/b) + f(n)
     = ...
     = a^k T(n/b^k) + Σ_{i=0}^{k-1} a^i f(n/b^i)        ← general term, write it
Stop when n/b^k = 1, i.e. k = log_b n. Then a^k = n^{log_b a}.
Plug in, evaluate the sum (geometric or constant per level), state Θ.
Worked (S26 Q1b): $T(n) = 64T(n/4) + n^3$. Each level: $64 \cdot (n/4)^3 = n^3$, so every level costs $n^3$. $k = \log_4 n$ levels plus leaves $64^{\log_4 n} = n^3$. Total $\Theta(n^3 \log n)$.

Worked (F25 Q1a): $T(n) = 4T(n/4) + n\log_4 n$. Level $i$ total: $4^i \cdot \frac{n}{4^i}\log_4\frac{n}{4^i} = n(\log_4 n - i)$. Sum over $i = 0..\log_4 n - 1$: $n \sum (\log_4 n - i) = n \cdot \frac{\log_4 n(\log_4 n + 1)}{2}$, plus $n$ leaves. $\Theta(n \log^2 n)$.
Recipe C: substitution for square roots and logs (S25 Q3, HW1 Q2c) If you see $T(\sqrt n)$, set $n = 2^m$ (so $\sqrt n = 2^{m/2}$) and define $S(m) = T(2^m)$. Then $T(\sqrt n) = S(m/2)$.
$T(n) = 2T(\sqrt n) + \log n$ becomes $S(m) = 2S(m/2) + m$, which is mergesort: $S(m) = \Theta(m \log m)$, so $T(n) = \Theta(\log n \cdot \log\log n)$.
$T(n) = T(\sqrt n) + \log n$ becomes $S(m) = S(m/2) + m = \Theta(m)$, so $T(n) = \Theta(\log n)$.

Why it matters Every recursive function you write, every MapReduce job, every "split the data across machines" plan has a recurrence behind it. Being able to look at a split and say "this is $n \log n$, that one is quadratic" is what separates a design that ships from one that gets rewritten.

Divide and conquer: split, solve, glue

Lectures 2 to 5, Tutorial 2, HW1 Q3 to Q5, HW2 Q1. The 10 to 20 point design question on every midterm.

Analogy You are looking for a word in a paper dictionary. You do not read from page one. You open the middle, decide which half, and throw the other half away. That is binary search, and it is why $\log n$ shows up: halving 1,000,000 pages takes only 20 steps. Divide and conquer is the same move applied to any problem where the answer to the whole can be glued from the answers to the halves plus a bit of work at the seam.
The three-line template for every D&C design answer 
Solve(P):                          // input: ..., output: ...  (define these!)
  if P is tiny: solve directly
  split P into pieces P1..Pa of size n/b
  R_i = Solve(P_i) for each piece
  combine R_1..R_a into the answer     // the seam: say exactly what happens here
Running time: T(n) = a T(n/b) + (cost of split + combine). Solve it. Done.
Graders want: the split, the combine, the recurrence, its solution. Correctness proof not required this year, but one sentence on why the combine is right costs nothing.

Binary search and its variants

Search(A, l, r, x):              // sorted A, returns index of x or nil
  if l > r: return nil
  m = floor((l+r)/2)
  if A[m] = x: return m
  if x < A[m]: return Search(A, l, m-1, x) else return Search(A, m+1, r, x)
T(n) = T(n/2) + O(1) = O(log n)
Loop version that finds the first element $\ge x$ (lower bound): 
l = 1; r = n+1
while l < r:
  m = floor((l+r)/2)
  if A[m] < x: l = m+1 else r = m
return l                           // n+1 if none
  • Rotated sorted array (Quiz 2): find the "drop" $k$ with $A[k] \gt A[k+1]$: if $A[m] \gt A[m+1]$ return $m$; if $A[m] \ge A[l]$ go right ($l = m+1$) else go left ($r = m-1$).
  • Last occurrence of $x$ in a sorted array (S25 Q6, S26 Q3): precondition $A[p] = x$. If $A[r] = x$ return $r$. Else $q = \lfloor (p+r)/2 \rfloor$; if $A[q] = x$ and $A[q+1] \gt x$ return $q$; if $A[q] = x$ recurse on $[q+1, r]$; else recurse on $[p, q-1]$. $O(\log n)$.
  • Distinct values with frequencies when there are $\Theta(\log n)$ distinct values: repeatedly find the last occurrence of $A[\text{start}]$, output value and length, jump past it. $m$ jumps times $O(\log n)$ each gives $O(\log^2 n)$. This exact question was on two of the three recent papers.
  • Galloping search when the answer is near the front at unknown position $k$: double an index $1, 2, 4, 8, \dots$ until you pass the target, then binary search the last interval. $O(\log k)$.
  • $k$-th smallest of two sorted arrays in $O(\log k)$ (F25 Q4): compare $A[k/2]$ with $B[k/2]$. The smaller one, together with everything before it in its array, cannot be the answer (at most $k-1$ things are below it), so discard those $k/2$ elements and look for the $(k/2)$-th smallest in what remains. If an array is shorter than $k/2$, treat the missing entry as $\infty$. $k$ halves each round, so $O(\log k)$ rounds of $O(1)$ work.
  • Local minimum in an $n \times n$ grid in $O(n)$ (HW2 Q1): look at the middle row, middle column, and the border (a "window frame with a cross", $O(n)$ cells). Take the minimum $M$ of those. If $M$ beats its four neighbours, done. Otherwise a smaller neighbour lies strictly inside one quadrant; recurse into that quadrant only. $T(n) = T(n/2) + O(n) = O(n)$. Why it works: the quadrant's boundary is all bigger than something inside it, so walking downhill inside can never leave.

Mergesort and counting inversions

Mergesort(A, l, r):
  if l >= r: return
  m = floor((l+r)/2)
  Mergesort(A, l, m); Mergesort(A, m+1, r)
  Merge(A, l, m, r)                // two pointers, sentinels ∞ at the ends, Θ(n)
T(n) = 2T(n/2) + n = Θ(n log n), for every input.
Inversions (pairs $i \lt j$ with $A[i] \gt A[j]$, a measure of "how unsorted"): count left, count right, count crossing pairs during the merge. When the element from the right half is placed, every element still waiting in the left half is bigger than it, so add that count in one step. Same recurrence, $\Theta(n \log n)$.

Maximum subarray

Four speeds for the same problem, a favourite "improve this" question.
IdeaTime
Try every $(i, j)$, sum from scratch$\Theta(n^3)$
Try every $(i, j)$, extend the running sum$\Theta(n^2)$

Stock profit (buy then sell) reduces to this by taking daily differences. Max product subarray (Tutorial 2) needs both the max and the min prefix and suffix products, because two negatives multiply to a positive.

Multiplication and the "fewer subproblems" trick

  • Integer multiplication: naive split gives 4 half-size products, $T = 4T(n/2) + n = \Theta(n^2)$, no gain. Karatsuba uses 3: $(a_1 + a_0)(b_1 + b_0) - a_1 b_1 - a_0 b_0$ gives the middle term. $T = 3T(n/2) + n = \Theta(n^{\log_2 3}) = \Theta(n^{1.585})$.
  • Matrix multiplication: block split gives 8 products, $\Theta(n^3)$. Strassen uses 7: $\Theta(n^{\log_2 7}) = \Theta(n^{2.81})$.
  • Lesson for the exam: when leaves dominate, only $a$ matters. Trading one multiplication for a few additions is a win.

Design patterns from homework that could come back as exam variants

  • More than $n/3$ votes (HW1 Q4): at most 2 winners (three would exceed $n$). D&C: candidates of each half (at most 2 each) are the only possible candidates for the whole (if $x$ has more than a third overall it has more than a third in some half). Count the at most 4 candidates in $O(n)$. $T = 2T(n/2) + O(n) = O(n \log n)$. Majority ($\gt n/2$) is the same with one candidate per half.
  • $k$-near-sorted arrays (HW1 Q3): one stack pass removes at most $2k$ offenders in $O(n)$ (push if non-decreasing, else pop and discard both), sort the removed ones in $O(k \log k)$, merge in $O(n)$.
  • Stable point sets (HW1 Q5): sort by $x$, split by a vertical line, recurse, then project every point onto the line. $O(n \log n)$ time and $O(n \log n)$ added points because each level adds at most $n$ and there are $\log n$ levels.
  • Closest pair (Tutorial 2): sort by $x$, split, $d = \min(d_L, d_R)$, check the strip of width $2d$ sorted by $y$, each point against a constant number of neighbours. $O(n \log^2 n)$, or $O(n \log n)$ with presorting.
Why it matters Binary search is in every database index and every "git bisect" that finds the commit that broke production. Mergesort's merge is how distributed systems combine sorted shards. Karatsuba-style tricks are why your phone can do public-key cryptography on thousand-digit numbers.
Try it: given a sorted array of distinct integers, find an index $i$ with $A[i] = i$ in $O(\log n)$
$A[i] - i$ is non-decreasing (distinct integers increase by at least 1 each step). Binary search for the first index where $A[m] - m \ge 0$ and check equality. $O(\log n)$.
Try it: $n$ sorted arrays of $n$ elements each. Find the overall median in $O(n \log n)$? Hint: $k$-th smallest of two sorted arrays, repeated.
Pairwise merge is too slow. Better: any value in the union has a rank computable in $O(n \log n)$ by binary searching each array ($n$ arrays times $\log n$). Binary search on the value over the sorted union of candidate values, or use a heap-based $k$-way merge to the middle, $O(n^2 \log n)$. The exam version usually keeps it to two arrays.

Sorting: quicksort, selection, and the sorting zoo

Lecture 7, Tutorial 4, HW2 Q3, F25 Q1d. Know every algorithm's worst case, best case, space, stability, and when it is the right pick.

Analogy Mergesort is a careful librarian: split the pile in half, sort each half, then interleave. Quicksort is a bouncer: pick one book (the pivot), throw everything smaller to the left and bigger to the right, then deal with each side. The bouncer is faster in practice because the throwing is done in place, but if the bouncer keeps picking the smallest book as pivot, the line never shortens: that is the $n^2$ worst case. Picking the pivot at random makes a bad night vanishingly unlikely.
Partition (Lomuto) and quicksort 
Partition(A, l, r):                 // pivot x = A[r]; returns pivot's final index
  x = A[r]; i = l - 1
  for j = l to r-1:
    if A[j] <= x: i = i + 1; swap A[i], A[j]
  swap A[i+1], A[r]; return i + 1
Invariant: A[l..i] <= x | A[i+1..j-1] > x | unseen | x at r.   Θ(n), in place.

Quicksort(A, l, r):
  if l >= r: return
  m = Partition(A, l, r); Quicksort(A, l, m-1); Quicksort(A, m+1, r)
Best: $2T(n/2) + n = \Theta(n \log n)$. Worst (sorted input, last-element pivot): $T(n-1) + n = \Theta(n^2)$. Randomized pivot: expected $\Theta(n \log n)$ on every input.
The expected comparisons proof (write it from memory) Let $z_1 \lt \dots \lt z_n$ be the sorted values. $z_i$ and $z_j$ are compared iff the first pivot chosen from $\{z_i, \dots, z_j\}$ is $z_i$ or $z_j$ (any other pivot from that set separates them forever). That first pivot is uniform over $j - i + 1$ candidates, so $\Pr[X_{ij} = 1] = \frac{2}{j-i+1}$. Then $$E[\text{comparisons}] = \sum_{i \lt j} \frac{2}{j-i+1} \le \sum_{i=1}^{n} \sum_{d=1}^{n} \frac{2}{d} = n \cdot 2H_n = O(n \log n).$$ 3-way quicksort (HW2 Q3): with many equal keys, Lomuto degenerates to $\Theta(n^2)$ on an all-equal array. Fix: partition into less, equal, greater (Dutch flag: pointers lt, i, gt; smaller swaps to lt and advances both, bigger swaps to gt and does not advance i, equal advances i). Then the comparison probability becomes $\le 2/|Z'_{ij}|$ where $Z'$ also includes ties, still $O(n \log n)$.
Randomized selection: $k$-th smallest in expected $O(n)$ 
Select(A, l, r, k):
  if l = r: return A[l]
  pick random pivot, m = Partition; q = m - l + 1       // rank of pivot in A[l..r]
  if k = q: return A[m]
  if k < q: return Select(A, l, m-1, k) else return Select(A, m+1, r, k-q)
Good-pivot argument (reused in F25 Q3 and in the OS and probability courses): a pivot in the middle half is "good" with probability $\frac12$, and a good pivot throws away at least a quarter. Expected calls per good pivot is 2 (geometric). After $i$ good pivots the size is at most $(\frac34)^i n$. Expected total $\le \sum_i 2 (\frac34)^i n = 8n = O(n)$.
Tutorial 4 does the same with indicators: three cases for $i \lt j$ relative to $k$ give $\frac{2}{j-i+1}$, $\frac{2}{k-i+1}$, $\frac{2}{j-k+1}$, and each sums to $O(n)$.
Deterministic median-of-medians exists in $O(n)$; with a black-box linear median, selection is $T(n) = T(n/2) + O(n) = O(n)$ and the weighted median (Tutorial 3) is solved by halving around the median and moving weight.

Randomization: expectation without fear

Lecture 6, Tutorials 3 and 4, HW2 Q2. 10 to 15 points on every midterm, almost always a coupon collector or hiring variant.

Analogy Expected value is a fair price. If a lottery ticket pays 100 with probability $\frac{1}{10}$, its fair price is 10. The magic tool is linearity: the fair price of a bundle is the sum of fair prices, even when the tickets are correlated. So to count something complicated, break it into yes/no questions (indicators), price each one, and add.
The four-step recipe that answers every randomization question
  1. Name the quantity $X$ you want in expectation.
  2. Write $X$ as a sum of simpler things: $X = \sum X_i$. Often $X_i \in \{0, 1\}$ (indicator) or $X_i$ is a waiting time.
  3. Compute each $E[X_i]$: indicator gives $\Pr[X_i = 1]$; waiting time with success probability $p$ gives $\frac1p$ (geometric).
  4. Add them (linearity, no independence needed). Simplify with $H_n = \Theta(\log n)$ or a geometric sum. If asked for upper and lower bounds, bound the sum both ways (max term and half trick).

Other facts that show up as short questions
  • Fisher-Yates shuffle: for $i = 1..n$ swap $A[i]$ with $A[\text{Random}(1, i)]$. $O(n)$, every permutation equally likely (induction on $i$).
  • Majority element: random pick is the majority with probability $\gt \frac12$, verify in $O(n)$, expected $\lt 2$ rounds. Boyer-Moore does it deterministically in one pass with a counter.
  • Measure of a randomized algorithm: expected time on the worst-case input. The adversary picks the input, the algorithm picks the coins.
  • $E[XY] = E[X]E[Y]$ needs independence. $E[X + Y] = E[X] + E[Y]$ never does. Variance is not linear unless independent.
  • Tail formula for non-negative integer $X$: $E[X] = \sum_{i \ge 1} \Pr[X \ge i]$.
Why it matters Hash tables, load balancers, A/B tests, Bloom filters, and every "sample 1% of traffic" dashboard rest on exactly these calculations. The coupon collector tells you how many random probes a cache needs before it has seen every key; the birthday bound tells you how long your random IDs must be to avoid collisions.

Heaps, linear-time sorts, and lower bounds

Tutorial 4, HW2 Q4 and Q5, and 10 to 20 points on every recent midterm. The lecture slides for these are not in your folder; this tab is built from the tutorial, homework, and exam solutions.

Analogy A min-heap is a tournament bracket where the winner is always on top. Each parent beats both children. Nothing else is promised: siblings are unordered. Inserting a player means putting them at the next empty seat and letting them climb while they beat their parent (bubble up). Removing the champion means moving the last seat to the top and letting it fall while a child beats it (bubble down). Height is $\log n$, so every move is $O(\log n)$.
Array heap facts
  • Stored in $H[1..n]$: children of $i$ are $2i$ and $2i+1$, parent is $\lfloor i/2 \rfloor$. Complete tree, filled left to right.
  • Insert: append, bubble up. Extract-min: swap root with last, shrink, bubble down. Both $O(\log n)$. Find-min $O(1)$.
  • Decrease-key at a known position: bubble up, $O(\log n)$. If you need to find the position you need pointers back into the heap (S25 Q4 hint).
  • Build-heap in $O(n)$: bubble down every node from the last internal node to the root. A node at height $h$ costs $O(h)$ and there are at most $n / 2^{h+1}$ of them: $\sum_h h \cdot \frac{n}{2^{h+1}} = O(n)$ because $\sum h/2^h = 2$. F25 Q2c asks for this proof "from scratch": merge pairs of heaps of size $2^k - 1$ under a new root, each merge costs $O(k)$, and $\sum_k k 2^{m-k-1} \le n + 1$.
  • Heapsort: build-heap then extract-min $n$ times: $O(n \log n)$, in place, not stable.

Heap design questions and their standard answers
  • $k$-way merge of $N$ total elements (Tutorial 4): heap of the $k$ current heads, extract-min, push the next from that array. $O(N \log k)$, $O(k)$ space.
  • Running median (Tutorial 4): max-heap for the lower half, min-heap for the upper half, sizes differ by at most 1. Insert $O(\log n)$, median $O(1)$.
  • $k$-th smallest in a heap without modifying it (HW2 Q4): auxiliary min-heap of (value, index) seeded with the root; $k$ times extract-min and push its two children. $O(k \log k)$, $O(k)$ space. Why: an element cannot be among the $k$ smallest unless its parent is.
  • Largest $\lceil \log n \rceil$ numbers in $O(n \log\log n)$ (S26 Q4b): split into $n / \log n$ blocks of size $\log n$, sort each block in $O(\log n \log\log n)$ (total $O(n \log\log n)$), then heap-merge the block tops $\log n$ times. The block sorting dominates.
  • Min over $m$ sets with add in $O(\log m + \log n_i)$ (S25 Q4): one heap per set plus a top heap of set minima; keep a pointer from each set to its position in the top heap and update pointers on every swap.
  • Priority queue with deletion of arbitrary items: store positions in a dictionary, swap with last, then bubble up or down.
Counting sort and radix sort 
CountingSort(A, k):                 // keys in 1..k
  C[1..k] = 0; for each a in A: C[a]++
  prefix sums: C[v] = number of keys <= v
  for j = n down to 1: B[C[A[j]]] = A[j]; C[A[j]]--      // backwards keeps it stable
Θ(n + k).  Radix sort: counting sort on each digit from least significant to most, Θ(d(n+k)).

Strings of total length $n$ (HW2 Q5): padding to the max length can cost $\Omega(n^2)$ (one long string plus many short ones). Fix: bucket strings by length, then do the LSD passes from the longest position down, adding shorter strings in as their last position becomes active. Each pass costs the number of strings still active, total $O(n)$.
Decision trees and lower bounds
  • A comparison algorithm is a binary tree: each internal node is a comparison, each leaf an output. Running time on an input is the depth of its leaf. Worst case is the height.
  • A tree with $L$ leaves has height $\ge \log_2 L$. Sorting has $n!$ outputs, so height $\ge \log_2 n! = \Omega(n \log n)$. That is why no comparison sort beats mergesort.
  • Count outputs, take the log (F25 Q1b): outputting the $k$ smallest in order has at least $\binom{n}{k} k! = n(n-1)\cdots(n-k+1) \ge (n/2)^k$ outcomes for $k \le n/2$, so $\Omega(k \log n)$ comparisons.
  • Drawing a decision tree (S25 Q1): simulate the algorithm, branch on every comparison it makes, label leaves with the output. For the recursive minimum of 4 elements: compare $a_1 : a_2$, then $a_3 : a_4$, then the two winners.
  • Finding the minimum needs $n - 1$ comparisons (every non-minimum must lose once). Finding both min and max needs about $3n/2$.
Tutorial 5 (Oct 5 to 7): the newest material, most likely to be reskinned
  • Sliding-window maximum with a heap: store (value, index) pairs in a max-heap; for each new index insert it, then while the root's index has left the window, extract it (lazy deletion). Root is the answer. Each element is inserted and extracted at most once, but with lazy deletion alone the heap can grow to $\Theta(n)$ on increasing input, so the honest bound is $O(n \log n)$ time and $O(n)$ space; with a position map you delete the outgoing element eagerly and get $O(n \log W)$ time, $O(W)$ space. The monotonic deque does it in $O(n)$ worst case. State your assumption.
  • $d$-ary heaps: children of $i$ are $d(i-1)+2, \dots, di+1$; parent of $j$ is $\lfloor (j-2)/d \rfloor + 1$; height $\Theta(\log_d n)$; insert $O(\log_d n)$; extract-max $O(d \log_d n)$ because sinking must find the max of $d$ children. Larger $d$ helps when decrease-keys far outnumber extractions (Dijkstra with $d \approx m/n$). $d = n$ is a sorted array: insert $O(1)$, extract $O(n)$.
  • When is radix sort linear? With $w$-bit keys and base $k$: $T = \Theta(\lceil w / \log k \rceil (n + k))$. Best base is $k = n$. Linear iff $w = O(\log n)$ iff the key range is polynomial in $n$. Integers in $[0, n^3)$: 3 base-$n$ digits, $\Theta(n)$. Integers in $[1, n^{\ln n}]$: $w = \Theta(\log^2 n)$ so $\Theta(\log n)$ passes, $\Theta(n \log n)$, radix cannot beat comparison sorting there. Pure counting sort with $k = n^3$ costs $\Theta(n^3)$, which is the trap.
  • Why counting sort scans from the back: after prefix sums $C[v]$ is the last slot for value $v$; filling from the end keeps equal keys in input order (stability), and radix sort's correctness is an induction on digit positions whose step is exactly stability.
  • Sorting $n$ fractions $p_i/q_i$ with $p_i, q_i \in [1, n]$ in $O(n)$: distinct fractions differ by at least $1/n^2$ (numerator of the difference is a positive integer, denominator at most $n^2$), so the integer key $K_i = \lfloor n^2 p_i / q_i \rfloor \in [n, n^3]$ preserves order; radix sort the keys in base $n$. Floats fail for large $n$ because gaps below $2^{-52}$ collapse; reducing by gcd first costs $O(n \log n)$ and is unnecessary.
  • Merging two sorted $n$-arrays needs $\Omega(n)$ comparisons: $\binom{2n}{n}$ possible interleavings, so decision-tree height $\ge \log_2 \binom{2n}{n} = 2n - O(\log n)$.
  • Take-home from the tutorial: sort a $k$-nearly-sorted array (each element at most $k$ from its place) in $O(n \log k)$ with a min-heap of size $k+1$. This is Practice Midterm B question 5(c).
Why it matters Heaps are the engine of every task scheduler, Dijkstra's shortest path, and "top $k$ trending" features. Radix sort is why sorting a billion 32-bit integers is a job for seconds, not minutes. Lower bounds tell you when to stop optimizing: no one will ever write a faster general comparison sort than $n \log n$, so spend your time elsewhere.

Common ways to lose points

  • Writing $O$ when the question asks for the exact form $\Theta(n^\alpha \log^\beta n)$.
  • Forgetting the leaf level in a recursion tree (it is what makes $2T(n/2) + \sqrt n$ linear).
  • Using the Master Theorem when the question says not to.
  • Assuming independence to multiply probabilities when linearity would have done it.
  • A design answer with no running-time justification, or a subroutine with no stated input and output.
  • Drawing only the final heap when every intermediate tree was requested.
  • Stating a $\Theta$ bound when only one direction was argued.
