## STEP 0 — BEFORE YOU READ ANY QUESTION (first 5 min)

Write this skeleton on your answer sheet from memory:

```
INTRO:
  [Thesis — restate question as a claim]
  [Preview — "This essay will argue X by examining Y and Z."]

BODY 1:
  [Claim] → [Evidence/mechanism] → [Example: name + date]

BODY 2 (counterpoint or 2nd argument):
  [Claim] → [Evidence/mechanism] → [Example: name + date]

BODY 3 (optional, or the C-relation):
  [Claim] → [ties the two body arguments together / resolves tension]

CONCLUSION:
  [Restate thesis in new words]
  [One forward-pointing question]
```

Then write your sentence starter bank (below) so it's visible while you write.

## SENTENCE STARTERS — USE THESE VERBATIM

### Intro / Thesis
- "Campbell-Kelly's claim that [X] is doing two distinct kinds of work: [A] on the one hand, [B] on the other."
- "To a large extent, [X] — for three reasons. However, one important qualification: [Z]."
- "[X] is best understood not as [common assumption] but as [your argument]."
- "The lecture's line of thought connects [X] to [Y] through the mechanism of [concept]."

### Body — introducing an argument
- "The clearest example is [name + date + what happened]."
- "This is illustrated by [case]: [one-sentence explanation of mechanism]."
- "[Name]'s [thing] ([ year]) demonstrates this precisely: [explain why]."
- "Three mechanisms drove this: first, [X]; second, [Y]; third, [Z]."

### Body — counterpoint / complication
- "Conversely, [counterexample] shows the limits of this argument."
- "One might object that [X]. This is true — but [reason why it doesn't undo your thesis]."
- "This was not inevitable: [contingent event] nearly prevented it."
- "The unintended-vs-deliberate distinction matters here: [explain]."

### Connecting A to B (C-questions)
- "The relation between [A] and [B] is one of [causation / tension / analogy]."
- "Both cases follow the same logic: [X] — which is the concept of [agendas / appropriation / defining technology] in miniature."
- "What connects them is not [surface similarity] but [deeper structural pattern]."

### Conclusion
- "In sum, Campbell-Kelly's claim holds [to a large / limited] extent: [restate thesis]."
- "The deeper point is that [technology / narrative / institution] is never neutral — it serves [who] by doing [what]."
- "This raises the open question of whether [forward-pointing implication for today]."

### The historiographical sentence (10.0 lever — add to every answer)
- "This telling serves [X] agenda: [who benefits from this version of history]."
- "Recognising the agenda does not invalidate the narrative — it asks us to read it critically."

## EXAM STRATEGY — MINUTE BY MINUTE

| Min | What to do |
|---|---|
| 0–5 | Write template + starter bank on your sheet (from memory, above) |
| 5–12 | Read ALL 26 questions. Circle top 2 per type (top 3 for B). Do NOT write yet. |
| 12–20 | For each of your 4 chosen questions, jot 2–3 bullet ideas (examples that are clearest in your head). Pick the question per type where your examples are sharpest. |
| 20–28 | Outline all 4 answers (2 min each): thesis + 3 body beats + conclusion line. Finish ALL outlines before writing any full answer. |
| 28–128 | Write 4 answers, 25 min each. One complete before moving to next. |
| 128–135 | Edit sweep: chronology errors? CK phrase used? Name + student number on each page? Question label written? |

**The single most important rule:** Outline all 4 before writing any. That's what protects answer 4 from being rushed.

### Key Ideas — One Line Each

**Agendas** — histories are told to serve present interests. Who benefits from this telling?
→ Aiken invokes Babbage; McCorduck writes AI history as advocacy; AI winters narrative secures funding

**Appropriation** — technology repurposed for uses its designers never intended
→ Ferranti Mark I (science) → Nim (AI demo); SAGE (military) → SABRE (airlines); GPU (games) → AlexNet (AI)

**Defining Technology** (Bolter) — reshapes not just what we do but how we understand ourselves
→ Cybernetics (1960s): brain as feedback system; AI (2020s): intelligence as computation

**Trust** — technical superiority is never enough; social trust must be built
→ Hollerith: output made legible; UNIVAC: CBS election demo; IBM: "nobody ever got fired for buying IBM"

**Surveillance Capitalism** (Zuboff) — user behaviour as raw material → prediction products → sell to advertisers
→ Google AdWords; Netflix recommendation engine; Facebook social graph. User is the product, not the customer.

**AI Winters** — funding collapses after oversold promises
→ 1st winter: Lighthill Report 1973; 2nd winter: expert systems collapse ~1987–93
→ The narrative itself is an agenda: used to argue for sustained investment ("different this time")

**Three Traditions** (lecture) — computing's prehistory: Administration (Hollerith), Science (ballistics/physics), Process Control (Dutch waterworks). AI comes from the Science + Logic tradition, not Administration.

**Confluence of Three Desires** (CK on Internet) — Military (ARPANET, survivability) + Academic (email, sharing) + Commercial (WWW, commerce). Tensions between them persist today.

**"Watson rational but wrong; Eckert & Mauchly irrational but right"** — rational analysis of present conditions predicts the wrong future. Technology creates new markets; it doesn't just serve existing ones.

**Software Crisis** (1968 NATO conference) — large software projects late, over budget, buggy. Two responses: Dijkstra (European) = mathematical rigour/structured programming; IBM (American) = management + more people (Brooks: makes it worse).

**California Blend** (CK Ch 9–10) — counterculture (Brand, Homebrew Club) + engineering (Stanford, Xerox PARC) = personal computing. Contrast with IBM East Coast (hierarchical, suit-wearing). Apple = synthesis.

**Broadening the appeal** (CK Ch 10) — 1980s, three mechanisms: GUI (Macintosh 1984, originally Xerox PARC) + killer apps (Lotus 1-2-3, VisiCalc) + open architecture price competition (IBM PC + Compaq clones). NOT games (1983 video game crash).

## SOURCE GUIDE — LECTURES vs. CK BOOK

**A-questions = lectures only** (never cite CK for an A-question)
**B-questions = CK book only** (never cite lecture-only content for a B-question)
**C-questions = both** (this is literally the instruction — you must combine them)

| Source | What it covers | Key signal words |
|---|---|---|
| **Lectures (A)** | Three traditions; trust/Hollerith; Dutch Dinosaurs; metaphors ("electronic brain"); AI & education (Skinner/Papert); game culture/Nim; cybernetics/Bolter; AI winters; Web 2.0 & AI; surveillance capitalism as lecture theme; History of AI defining AI; prehistory of AI older than computing | "During the lecture…", "In the lecture it was explained…" |
| **CK book (B)** | Ch 4 Moore School; Ch 5 IBM/UNIVAC/Watson phrase; Ch 6 Mainframe/System 360; Ch 7 Whirlwind/SAGE/SABRE; Ch 8–9 Timesharing/BASIC/Whole Earth; Ch 10 Personal computers/GUI/broadening; Ch 11 Internet/three desires; Ch 12 Silicon Valley; Ch 13 Globalisation/offshoring; Ch 14 Google/Netflix/surveillance capitalism; Ch 15 Governance/GDPR | "Campbell-Kelly argues…", "According to Campbell-Kelly…", CK exact phrases |

**Practical rule:** If you're writing a C-answer, you need at least one named CK example (person/firm/date from the book) AND one lecture concept (agendas, appropriation, Bolter, etc.).

## MAIN THEMES — WHAT EVERY QUESTION IS REALLY ASKING

Every question in this course is secretly about one or more of these themes. Identify the theme first, then apply your examples.

| Theme | What it means | Go-to examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Trust** | New tech needs social/institutional trust, not just technical merit | Hollerith 1890 (made output legible); UNIVAC CBS 1952 (spectacle); IBM "nobody got fired" |
| **Agendas** | Histories serve present interests — who benefits from this telling? | Aiken→Babbage; McCorduck *Machines Who Think*; AI winter narrative; "two boys in a garage" myth |
| **Appropriation** | Technology repurposed for unintended uses — where the real value emerges | Ferranti→Nim (AI); SAGE→SABRE (civilian); GPU→AlexNet (deep learning); WWW→e-commerce |
| **Defining technology** (Bolter) | Reshapes how we understand ourselves, not just what we do | Cybernetics 1960s (brain as feedback system); AI 2020s (intelligence as computation) |
| **Surveillance capitalism** (Zuboff) | Behaviour as raw material; user is the product | Google AdWords 2000; Netflix recommendation; Facebook social graph |
| **Broadening the appeal** | Making tech accessible to non-specialists | GUI (Mac 1984); killer apps (VisiCalc, Lotus); open architecture + clones (Compaq 1982) |
| **Confluence of desires** | Multiple groups with different goals converge on one technology | Internet: military + academic + commercial; Personal computing: counterculture + engineering |
| **AI winters** | Funding collapse after overselling — and the narrative itself is an agenda | Lighthill 1973; expert systems collapse 1987–93; "different this time" rhetoric |
| **Prejudice in histories** | Anglo-American / male-dominated narratives exclude others | Polish codebreakers (Rejewski broke Enigma before Turing); ENIAC operators were women; Dutch computing |
| **Three traditions** | Computing came from three separate roots that converged | Administration (Hollerith); Science (ballistics, physics); Process Control (Dutch waterworks) |
| **Company culture** | Silicon Valley culture ≠ IBM culture ≠ European academic culture | Flat hierarchy + stock options + fail fast (SV) vs. suits + hierarchy + government funding (EU/IBM) |

## THE HISTORIOGRAPHICAL SENTENCE (add to every answer for 10.0)

End or begin a body paragraph with:
> *"This telling serves [X] agenda: [Aiken / AI advocates / IBM / Silicon Valley myth-makers] benefit from [this version of history] because [it legitimises their current project / secures funding / establishes priority]."*

Beckers is a historian of science. This move — identifying whose agenda a narrative serves — is the single most valued analytical move in the course.
