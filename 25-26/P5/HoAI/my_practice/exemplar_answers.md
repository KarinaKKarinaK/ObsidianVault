## A1. History of AI
**Question:** Discuss in what ways history of AI helps defining AI.

**Outline:**
- Thesis: AI has no stable definition → its history is one of the only tools we have to define it
- Body 1: The naming moment — McCarthy coins "AI" at Dartmouth 1956, deliberately breaking from Wiener's "cybernetics" → the name is itself a definitional act
- Body 2: AI winters trace the definitions — each winter = a definition that failed. Symbolic reasoning (1st winter, Lighthill 1973); expert systems (2nd winter, 1987–93); each collapse redefines AI more narrowly
- Body 3: Defining technology (Bolter) — AI reshapes how we understand intelligence itself → history is circular: AI develops → definition of intelligence changes → AI is redefined
- Conclusion + historiographical sentence: McCorduck writes AI history as advocacy → history defines AND legitimises AI simultaneously
- Key names/dates: McCarthy, Wiener, Dartmouth 1956, Lighthill Report 1973, MYCIN, McCorduck, Bolter

**Exemplar answer:**

The history of AI helps define AI precisely because AI has never had a stable definition of its own. At each moment in its history, what counted as "artificial intelligence" shifted — and tracing those shifts is one of the few reliable tools for understanding what the field actually is.

The clearest starting point is the naming moment. In 1956, John McCarthy coined the term "Artificial Intelligence" at the Dartmouth Conference — deliberately distinguishing his programme from Norbert Wiener's "cybernetics." This was not a neutral choice: it was a definitional act. By choosing "AI" over "cybernetics," McCarthy defined the field as concerned with formal symbolic reasoning rather than biological feedback systems. The history of that choice reveals a definition.

More powerfully, the AI winters trace the definitions across time. The first winter (1973) followed the Lighthill Report, which concluded that AI had failed to deliver on promises of machine translation and general reasoning. What was promised is what AI was defined as at that moment. The second winter (late 1980s) followed the collapse of expert systems — programs like MYCIN that encoded human expertise in rigid rules. Their brittleness revealed the limits of that definition: AI as rule-following. Each failure redraws the boundary.

Finally, AI functions as a defining technology in Bolter's sense — it reshapes how we understand intelligence itself. As machines achieve tasks once thought uniquely human, the definition of human intelligence shifts. History tracks this moving target.

The deeper point is that AI's history is written to advocate for the field. Pamela McCorduck's *Machines Who Think* is the clearest example — history that simultaneously defines and legitimises AI. Recognising this agenda does not invalidate the history; it asks us to read it critically.

## A2. AI and Education
**Question:** To what extent was the 1950s/60s hype of programmed instruction instrumental in the visibility of AI? Make a comparison to the 1970s/80s hype of LOGO.

**Outline:**
- Thesis: Both made AI visible beyond the lab — but in opposite ways; programmed instruction = institutional efficiency; LOGO = individual liberation
- Body 1: Programmed instruction (Skinner, 1950s–60s) — behaviourist, top-down, computers as teaching machines → gave AI a mass application → attracted government/military funding → BUT defined AI narrowly as rule-following automation
- Body 2: LOGO (Papert, MIT, 1967) — constructivist (Piaget), bottom-up, turtle graphics → AI as creative humanistic tool → huge 1970s–80s hype, schools bought computers for LOGO → BUT learning outcomes disappointing, faded by late 1980s
- Body 3: Both are hype cycles mirroring AI winters; key difference = opposite agendas (institutional control vs. countercultural liberation); both show AI visibility requires attaching to something people already care about
- Conclusion + historiographical sentence: advocates (Skinner/Papert) wrote these histories — educational AI hype was always also a hype about pedagogy
- Key names/dates: Skinner, Papert, MIT 1967, Piaget, Apple II, BBC Micro, "teaching machine"

**Exemplar answer:**

Both programmed instruction and LOGO made AI visible to the public and to funders — but through opposite agendas. Programmed instruction sold AI as a tool of institutional efficiency; LOGO sold it as a tool of individual liberation. Together they show that AI's public profile has always depended on the politics of those promoting it.

Programmed instruction emerged from B.F. Skinner's behaviourist learning theory in the 1950s and 60s. The model was simple: break knowledge into small steps, present each to the student, give immediate feedback. Computers were ideal "teaching machines" — tireless, consistent, scalable. This gave AI its first mass application, and the visibility that came with it. Governments and military funders could see a clear use case. However, the visibility came at a definitional cost: AI was framed as sophisticated rule-following, not genuine intelligence. Critics argued it dehumanised learning and reduced education to stimulus-response.

LOGO, developed by Seymour Papert at MIT in 1967, drew on Piaget's constructivism — children learn by making, not receiving. LOGO let children program a turtle to draw shapes, discovering geometry and recursion through play. In the 1970s and 80s, the hype was enormous: schools bought Apple IIs and BBC Micros specifically to run LOGO. It made AI seem creative and humanising rather than mechanical. Yet here too the hype outran the evidence. Measured learning outcomes were modest, and LOGO faded by the late 1980s.

The pattern is identical in both cases: a compelling educational agenda amplifies AI's visibility, followed by disappointment when promises outrun delivery — a miniature AI winter in each case. The difference is political: Skinner served institutional control; Papert served countercultural liberation. Same technology, opposite agendas.

## A3. Prehistory of AI
**Question:** During the lecture, a prehistory of AI was compared to a prehistory of computing. What were the events these histories had in common?

**Outline:**
- Thesis: Prehistories of AI and computing are largely separate traditions (logic/mathematics vs. administration) but share three key convergence points
- Body 1: Babbage's Analytical Engine (1837) — claimed by both as ancestor; Ada Lovelace's notes (1843) include proto-algorithm; both fields use him for agendas
- Body 2: Turing (1936–1950) — 1936 paper on computability foundational for computing theory; 1950 "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" foundational for AI; the one figure where both histories fully overlap
- Body 3: WWII as shared context — Colossus (codebreaking, Turing) + ENIAC (ballistics, Moore School) both use wartime infrastructure; cybernetics (Wiener 1948) emerges from same milieu and bridges both traditions
- Conclusion: They formally merge at Dartmouth 1956 — before that, mostly parallel histories from different intellectual roots (logic/philosophy vs. administration/engineering)
- Key names/dates: Babbage 1837, Lovelace 1843, Turing 1936 + 1950, ENIAC 1945, Wiener 1948, Dartmouth 1956

**Exemplar answer:**

The prehistories of AI and computing come from different intellectual traditions — computing from administration and engineering, AI from logic and philosophy of mind — but they share three important convergence points before formally merging at Dartmouth in 1956.

The first is Babbage's Analytical Engine (1837). Babbage's design for a programmable mechanical calculator is claimed as a precursor by both computing historians (stored-program concept, conditional branching) and AI historians (a machine that follows logical instructions). Ada Lovelace's notes (1843), which describe how the engine might compose music if given rules, are cited by both traditions as the first algorithm. Crucially, both fields use Babbage instrumentally — Howard Aiken invoked him for computing funding; AI advocates invoke him to show the field's long lineage. The same figure serves two agendas.

The second convergence point is Alan Turing. His 1936 paper on computability — introducing the Turing machine — is foundational for computer science. His 1950 paper "Computing Machinery and Intelligence," introducing the imitation game, is foundational for AI. Turing is the single figure where both histories most fully overlap, which is why he can be called "father" of both fields — though that claim is itself contested and agenda-driven.

The third is the shared wartime context of the 1940s. ENIAC (Moore School, 1945) and Colossus (Bletchley, Turing) both emerged from WWII military needs, using overlapping engineering communities. Norbert Wiener's Cybernetics (1948), which proposed brain-machine equivalence, emerged from the same milieu and provided the intellectual bridge between computing and AI research.

Before Dartmouth 1956, the two traditions ran mostly in parallel. Their convergence there — when McCarthy coined "Artificial Intelligence" — is the founding moment of the field as we know it.

## B1. Moore School
**Question:** "Computing was not performed for its own sake, but always as a means to an end." Campbell-Kelly claims this with regard to Moore School. Explain that remark.

**Outline:**
- Thesis: Moore School computing was entirely instrumental — the goal was always ballistics/military output, never computers as an end in themselves
- Body 1: ENIAC (1945) — built for US Army ballistics tables; Eckert & Mauchly + Goldstine; the computer was the tool, firing tables were the product
- Body 2: EDVAC — Von Neumann joins, writes First Draft (stored-program concept); again military means; bitter dispute over credit shows the people involved cared about the ends (military calculation) not the machine itself
- Body 3: Moore School Lectures (1946) — knowledge disseminated to UK/Europe; even this was instrumental — spreading capability for national defence + scientific calculation. Wilkes → EDSAC; Newman/Williams → Manchester Baby
- Conclusion: The "means to an end" claim is CK's argument that early computing had no independent identity — it was always in service of something else. This changed only when computers became commercial products (Ch 5 onwards)
- Key names/dates: Eckert & Mauchly, Goldstine, Von Neumann, ENIAC 1945, EDVAC, Moore School Lectures 1946, Wilkes/EDSAC 1949

**Exemplar answer:**

Campbell-Kelly's claim that "computing was not performed for its own sake, but always as a means to an end" captures a fundamental truth about the Moore School: the computer was never the goal. It was the instrument through which other goals — military, scientific, administrative — were pursued.

The clearest example is ENIAC (1945). Eckert and Mauchly built it at the University of Pennsylvania with Army Ballistics Research Laboratory funding. Its purpose was to compute artillery firing tables — the enormous sets of calculations that told gunners how to aim in different atmospheric conditions. The computer was the means; the firing tables were the end. ENIAC was not built because anyone thought computing was intrinsically valuable. It was built because calculating by hand was too slow.

The same logic applies to EDVAC, the follow-on project. When Von Neumann joined and wrote the famous First Draft of a Report on EDVAC (1945) — introducing the stored-program concept — the context remained military calculation. The bitter dispute over authorship that followed (Eckert and Mauchly felt Von Neumann had stolen credit) shows that the people involved cared intensely about priority, but the underlying driver was still instrumental: building better tools for military computation.

The Moore School Lectures of 1946 extended this logic internationally. By disseminating the stored-program design to engineers from the UK, Europe, and across the US, the Moore School spread not a vision of computing as a field, but a set of techniques useful for scientific and military calculation. Maurice Wilkes returned to Cambridge and built EDSAC (1949) to serve Cambridge researchers.

CK's point is that computing only acquired an independent identity — a "field" with its own agenda — later, when it became a commercial product. The Moore School was its birthplace, not its coming-of-age.

## B2. Netflix
**Question:** To what extent does Netflix illustrate the rise of Web 2.0? In what sense does Campbell-Kelly relate Netflix to the rise of surveillance capitalism?

**Outline:**
- Thesis: Netflix is a near-perfect illustration of Web 2.0 logic AND surveillance capitalism — user behaviour is the product, not just the content
- Body 1: Web 2.0 definition — user-generated engagement, personalisation, platform logic, network effects; Netflix fits: viewing behaviour drives the recommendation engine, which drives more viewing, which generates more data
- Body 2: Surveillance capitalism (Zuboff) — behaviour as raw material → prediction products → sold/used to influence future behaviour; Netflix: every second of viewing, every pause, every abandoned episode = behavioural data → used to commission content (House of Cards), optimise thumbnails, maximise watch time
- Body 3: The qualification — Netflix is subscription-based, not ad-funded (originally), so the "sold to advertisers" part differs from Google. But since 2022 Netflix has an ad-supported tier. And the underlying logic — extract behaviour, use it to maximise engagement — is identical. User pays with money AND data.
- Conclusion: Netflix shows surveillance capitalism is not just about advertising — it is about behavioural modification through data. The goal is engagement maximisation, which is behaviour modification.
- Key names/dates: Netflix founded 1997, streaming 2007; Zuboff *Surveillance Capitalism* 2019; Web 2.0 mid-2000s; House of Cards data-driven commissioning; ad-supported tier 2022

**Exemplar answer:**

Netflix illustrates the rise of Web 2.0 almost perfectly, and Campbell-Kelly uses it to show that surveillance capitalism extends well beyond advertising — it is a logic of behavioural extraction that applies wherever digital platforms interact with users at scale.

Web 2.0, which emerged in the mid-2000s, is characterised by user-generated content, personalisation, and the use of behavioural data to improve the platform. Netflix fits this model precisely. Every second of viewing, every pause, every abandoned episode, every re-watch generates data. This data feeds the recommendation engine, which suggests the next programme, which generates more viewing, which produces more data. The platform and the user are in a continuous feedback loop — the defining structure of Web 2.0.

The connection to surveillance capitalism — as described by Shoshana Zuboff — is direct. Zuboff defines surveillance capitalism as a business model in which human experience is collected as raw material, processed into prediction products, and used to influence behaviour. Netflix does exactly this: it used viewing data to commission House of Cards before a single episode was filmed, having determined algorithmically that the combination of director, cast, and genre would succeed. The recommendation algorithm does not merely reflect taste — it shapes it, nudging users toward content that maximises engagement and subscriber retention.

One qualification is worth noting: Netflix was originally subscription-funded, not advertising-funded, which distinguishes it from Google's AdWords model. However, the structural logic is identical — extract behaviour, use it to modify future behaviour — and since 2022 Netflix has introduced an ad-supported tier, closing even that gap.

Campbell-Kelly's argument is that Netflix shows surveillance capitalism is not an aberration of one company but the defining logic of the platform economy: the user is always, in some sense, the product.

## B3. IBM 1401
**Question:** Campbell-Kelly explains the success of the IBM 1401 as the result of good technology and great marketing. Explain his line of thought.

**Outline:**
- Thesis: The IBM 1401 succeeded because it combined genuine technical innovation with IBM's unmatched ability to make that technology legible and desirable to business customers
- Body 1: The technology — IBM 1401 (1959), transistorised, small enough for mid-size businesses, IBM 1403 chain printer (fastest in the world), RPG (Report Program Generator — non-programmers could generate business reports without writing code). Forecast: 1,000 units. Actual: 12,000+
- Body 2: The marketing — IBM's leasing model (no upfront cost, lower risk), service contracts, salesforce trained to speak to business needs not technical specs, positioned as "data processing" tool (familiar framing) not threatening "electronic brain." IBM brand = trust
- Body 3: The combined effect — RPG is the key synthesis: it made the technology usable without technical expertise (good technology) AND it was sold as a business productivity tool not a computer (great marketing). This is CK's "broadening" argument before the 1980s — the 1401 broadened computing into mid-size business
- Conclusion: The 1401 is CK's "Model T of computing" — it made computing ordinary. Neither the technology nor the marketing alone was sufficient; their combination is the argument.
- Key names/dates: IBM 1401 (1959), IBM 1403 chain printer, RPG, leasing model, "Model T of computing," 12,000 units sold, System/360 (1964) as successor

**Exemplar answer:**

Campbell-Kelly's account of the IBM 1401's success rests on a precise argument: that technical innovation and marketing were not separate contributions but mutually reinforcing. Each made the other more effective.

The technical case is strong. Launched in 1959, the IBM 1401 was transistorised — smaller, cooler, and more reliable than its vacuum-tube predecessors — and priced within reach of mid-size businesses, not just large corporations. Its key technological feature was the IBM 1403 chain printer, the fastest printer in the world at the time, which made it immediately valuable for any organisation that needed to produce large volumes of reports. More importantly, IBM introduced RPG — Report Program Generator — which allowed non-programmers to generate business reports without writing code. This was a genuine technical innovation: it removed the requirement for specialist programmers and put computing within reach of ordinary business operations.

But technology alone did not explain the 12,000 units sold against a forecast of 1,000. IBM's marketing infrastructure was equally decisive. The leasing model — customers paid monthly rather than purchasing outright — reduced financial risk and made adoption easier to justify internally. IBM's salesforce was trained not to sell computers but to sell solutions to business problems: payroll, inventory, billing. The product was framed as "data processing," a familiar and reassuring concept, rather than the threatening "electronic brain" of newspaper headlines. IBM's service contracts meant customers were never left alone with a machine they didn't understand.

Campbell-Kelly calls the 1401 the "Model T of computing" — the machine that made computing ordinary. The comparison is apt: like Ford's Model T, its significance was not technical supremacy but the combination of good-enough technology with a distribution and support system that made it accessible at scale.

## C1. Histories & Appropriation
**Question:** The traditional history of computing makes use of the concept of appropriation. To what extent does that concept also apply to a history of AI?

**Outline:**
- Thesis: Appropriation applies to AI history to a large extent — AI has consistently developed by repurposing computing infrastructure designed for other purposes, and this is the primary mechanism linking the two histories
- Body 1 (computing appropriation): Define appropriation. CK example: SAGE (military air defence) → SABRE (American Airlines reservations, 1957–64) → credit cards → ATMs. Technology designed for one end repurposed for civilian use; value emerges in use not design.
- Body 2 (AI appropriation): Same mechanism. Ferranti Mark I (scientific computer) → Strachey plays Nim (1951, first AI demo); GPU (designed for video games, 1990s) → Hinton's AlexNet (2012) triggers deep learning revolution; web-scraped data (ImageNet, 2009) trains computer vision models
- Body 3 (the qualification): One difference — in computing, appropriation often moved from military to civilian. In AI, appropriation also moves from one *research tradition* to another (logic → statistics → neural networks). Each AI paradigm shift involves appropriating tools from adjacent fields (lambda calculus → LISP; linear algebra → neural networks). This is a broader form of appropriation.
- Conclusion + historiographical sentence: Appropriation applies fully to AI history — and recognising this challenges the narrative that AI developed through a single coherent research programme. The history of AI is a history of borrowed tools.
- Key names/dates: Strachey/Nim 1951, SAGE→SABRE, McCarthy/LISP 1958, lambda calculus (Church 1936), GPU/AlexNet 2012, ImageNet 2009, Hinton

**Exemplar answer:**

Appropriation — the repurposing of a technology for uses its designers never intended — applies to the history of AI to a very large extent. In fact, AI's development can be understood almost entirely through a series of appropriations of computing infrastructure built for other purposes.

In Campbell-Kelly's history of computing, appropriation is most visible in the real-time computing chapter. SAGE, the military air-defence network (operational 1958), was designed to track Soviet bombers. It was militarily obsolete almost immediately — ICBMs bypassed its logic — but it trained engineers, developed CRT displays and core memory, and gave IBM the expertise to build SABRE (1957–64), the first civilian real-time reservations system for American Airlines. Military technology was appropriated for commercial use; the value emerged not from the intended application but from an unintended one.

AI history follows an identical pattern. Christopher Strachey programmed the Ferranti Mark I — a scientific computer — to play Nim in 1951. The machine was designed for numerical calculation; Strachey appropriated it to demonstrate symbolic reasoning. This act of appropriation is widely regarded as one of the founding demonstrations of AI. More recently, the GPU — designed by NVIDIA for video game rendering in the 1990s — was appropriated by Geoffrey Hinton's team to train AlexNet in 2012, triggering the deep learning revolution. Without games hardware, modern AI would not exist in its current form.

A qualification is worth noting: AI appropriation also operates within research traditions, not just across hardware uses. McCarthy appropriated Church's lambda calculus (1936) to build LISP (1958); contemporary AI appropriated linear algebra and statistics from mathematics. This is a broader form of appropriation — borrowing conceptual tools, not just physical machines.

The concept therefore applies fully, and with an important implication: AI did not develop through a single coherent research programme. It is an accumulation of borrowed tools applied to new ends. The history of AI is, in this sense, a history of appropriation.

## C2. Defining Technology
**Question:** Discuss two 21st century examples of AI as a defining technology (in Bolter's sense).

**Outline:**
- Thesis: AI in the 21st century meets Bolter's criterion — it reshapes not just what we do but how we understand human nature itself — with two clear examples: AI in creative production and AI in medical diagnosis
- Define Bolter briefly: defining technology = reshapes self-understanding across domains, provides a new cultural vocabulary; clock (17th c.) → humans as clockwork; computer/cybernetics (1960s) → brain as information processor
- Body 1: Generative AI and creativity (2020s) — ChatGPT, DALL-E, etc. force redefinition of creativity, authorship, originality. "AI-generated" is a new cultural category. If a machine can write poetry and pass bar exams, what makes human expression distinct? Legal systems, education, art markets all being restructured around this question. New vocabulary: "hallucination," "prompt engineering," "training data" enter everyday language — exactly Bolter's criterion.
- Body 2: AI in medicine / AlphaFold (2020) — DeepMind's AlphaFold solved the protein-folding problem that biology had worked on for 50 years. This reshapes how scientists understand the relationship between computation and biological truth. More broadly, AI diagnostic tools (radiology, pathology) force redefinition of medical expertise and the doctor-patient relationship.
- Body 3 (complication): Bolter requires *durable* reshaping. AI's status is not yet settled — we may be in another hype cycle before a third winter. The defining technology claim is strong but provisional. Worth flagging.
- Conclusion: AI has strong claims to be the defining technology of the early 21st century. The critical question is whether the cultural transformation will persist — or whether, like cybernetics, it will be absorbed into the next paradigm.
- Key names/dates: Bolter *Turing's Man* 1984; ChatGPT 2022; AlphaFold 2020; DeepMind; Wiener/cybernetics 1948 as the previous defining technology

**Exemplar answer:**

J. David Bolter defines a "defining technology" as one that reshapes not just what people do but how they understand themselves and their world — providing a cultural metaphor for human nature. The clock made humans clockwork; the steam engine made society an energy system; cybernetics made the brain an information processor. AI in the 21st century has strong claims to be the current defining technology, illustrated most clearly by two examples.

The first is generative AI and the redefinition of creativity. The emergence of systems like ChatGPT (2022) and DALL-E forces a cultural renegotiation of what creativity, authorship, and originality mean. If a machine can write legal briefs, compose music, and generate images indistinguishable from human work, the question "what is uniquely human about human expression?" becomes urgent across law, education, art markets, and philosophy. New vocabulary — "hallucination," "prompt engineering," "AI-generated" — has entered everyday language. This spread of AI terminology into cultural discourse is precisely Bolter's criterion: the technology provides a new vocabulary that restructures understanding beyond the technical domain.

The second is AI in biological science, exemplified by DeepMind's AlphaFold (2020), which solved the protein-folding problem — predicting a protein's 3D structure from its amino acid sequence — that structural biology had worked on for fifty years. This reshapes how scientists understand the relationship between computation and biological truth. AI is no longer just a tool for biology; it is redefining what biological knowledge looks like and who can produce it.

One qualification is necessary. Bolter requires durable reshaping — the clock and steam engine transformed self-understanding for centuries. AI's status is not yet settled; we may be in a period of hype before another winter. Whether the current transformation persists or is absorbed into the next paradigm remains the open question of the 2020s.

## C3. Digital Culture
**Question:** To what extent is "digital culture" a suitable way of describing twenty-first century culture?

**Outline:**
- Thesis: "Digital culture" is suitable to a large extent but risks obscuring more than it reveals — it describes the medium, not the meaning, and flattens enormous variation in who participates and on what terms
- Body 1 (it works): Digital technology has restructured core cultural practices — music (vinyl → streaming, Spotify), journalism (print → social media virality), banking (ATMs + algorithmic credit scoring), education (MOOCs, AI tutors). The logic is consistent: platformisation, data collection, personalisation, network effects. CK: Google/Netflix/Facebook as examples. Web 2.0 = user-generated content + behavioural data as the defining cultural logic.
- Body 2 (it doesn't fully work): "Digital culture" implies universality — but the digital divide (CK Ch 13, globalisation) means billions lack meaningful access. Describing 21st-century culture as "digital" centres the experience of the connected Global North. Furthermore, "digital" describes the medium but not the content — conflict, love, power, inequality are not digital phenomena that happen to use digital infrastructure.
- Body 3 (the synthesis): The more precise framing might be "surveillance capitalist culture" (Zuboff) or "platform culture" — capturing not just the digital medium but the specific economic logic (behavioural data as raw material) that shapes how digital culture actually operates. "Digital" is a necessary but insufficient descriptor.
- Conclusion + historiographical sentence: The term "digital culture" is useful as a starting point but serves the agenda of those who benefit from presenting digital technology as culturally neutral infrastructure. Asking *whose* digital culture, governed *how*, and with what exclusions, is the more productive question.
- Key names/dates: Zuboff, Web 2.0, Spotify/Netflix/Facebook, digital divide (CK Ch 13), GDPR 2018, Bolter (defining technology), ImageNet/AI as cultural infrastructure

**Exemplar answer:**

"Digital culture" is a suitable description of twenty-first century culture to a significant extent — but it risks describing the medium while obscuring the meaning. Used critically, it is a valuable concept; used uncritically, it flattens the specific economic logic and structural inequalities that shape how digital technology actually operates.

The case for the term is strong. Digital technology has restructured the core practices of cultural life in the twenty-first century. Music moved from physical media to streaming platforms; journalism from print gatekeeping to algorithmic virality; banking from physical branches to data-driven credit scoring; education from fixed curricula to personalised digital delivery. What these transformations share is a consistent logic: platformisation, continuous data collection, personalisation through algorithms, and network effects that concentrate power in a small number of firms. Campbell-Kelly's chapters on Google, Netflix, and the Internet document this shift comprehensively. In this sense, "digital culture" names something real.

However, two problems limit the term's usefulness. First, it implies universality. The digital divide — documented in Campbell-Kelly's chapter on globalisation — means that large portions of the global population lack reliable internet access, affordable devices, or the digital literacy to participate meaningfully. Describing twenty-first century culture as "digital" centres the experience of the connected Global North and renders others invisible.

Second, "digital" describes the medium, not the logic. The more precise term might be "surveillance capitalist culture" — Shoshana Zuboff's framework captures what is distinctive: human behaviour as raw material, prediction products sold to influence future behaviour, engagement maximisation as the organising principle. This is not just a digital phenomenon; it is a specific economic arrangement that happens to use digital infrastructure.

"Digital culture" is a useful starting point, but it serves the agenda of presenting technology as culturally neutral. The productive questions are: whose digital culture, governed how, and with what exclusions?
