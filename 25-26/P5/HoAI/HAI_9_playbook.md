### Ch 5 — The Computer Becomes a Business Machine (1948–1958) 🔴

**Story arc:** The computer started as a one-off scientific instrument. Three kinds of companies competed to turn it into a product: (1) **electronics firms** (RCA, GE, Honeywell, Raytheon) had the technical chops but not the business sense; (2) **business machine firms** (IBM, Remington Rand, NCR, Burroughs) had the customers and sales force but were slow to embrace electronics; (3) **startups** (Eckert-Mauchly Computer Corp, ERA) had vision but no capital.

Eckert and Mauchly built **UNIVAC** for the **Census Bureau** (delivered 1951). Their company was undercapitalised; Remington Rand acquired them in 1950. In **November 1952** UNIVAC predicted the Eisenhower landslide on **CBS** — a PR triumph that put "computer" in every American living room. But Remington Rand had no idea what to do with it: they didn't invest, didn't market, didn't follow up. Eckert and Mauchly were **irrational but right** — they bet on electronics with no business plan and won the technology race.

IBM, under **Thomas Watson Sr.**, was deeply sceptical of electronics. He thought punched-card tabulators were the future. The **SSEC** (1948) was built mostly as PR — a giant hybrid machine in the Madison Avenue window. Watson Sr. was **rational but wrong** — he applied sound business logic to the wrong premise. Watson Jr. and the electronics engineers slowly turned IBM around. The **IBM 650** (1954, Magnetic Drum Calculator) was IBM's first true commercial computer success — over 2,000 sold by 1962. By **end of 1950s** the market was "IBM and the seven dwarfs" (Sperry Rand, Honeywell, RCA, GE, Control Data, Burroughs, NCR).

**Exact CK phrases:** "Watson was rational but wrong, Eckert and Mauchly were irrational but right" · "IBM and the seven dwarfs" · the **EDP** (Electronic Data Processing) market

**Most likely question:** Explain the Watson/Eckert phrase → frame as the paradox that good business logic led IBM down the wrong path, while reckless visionaries built the future. Mention Census Bureau, UNIVAC on CBS, Remington Rand failure to capitalise, IBM 650 as the late catch-up.

### Ch 6 — The Maturing of the Mainframe (1959–1971)

**Story arc:** IBM consolidated dominance. The **IBM 1401** (1959) was the chapter's hero: a small, transistorised, lease-priced computer with the **IBM 1403 chain printer** (fastest in the world) and **RPG** (Report Program Generator — non-programmers could generate reports). Forecast 1,000 units; sold 12,000+. The 1401 broke computing out of the corporate raised-floor priesthood.

The "seven dwarfs" used three survival strategies: (1) **IBM-compatible clones** (RCA Spectra 70), (2) **own compatible families** (Honeywell, Burroughs, NCR), (3) **niche specialisation** (Control Data → scientific supercomputers; GE → timesharing).

**System/360** (announced **April 1964**) was IBM's biggest bet ever. One compatible family from small to large machines — risk everything on architecture. Technically conservative, commercially revolutionary. By 1970–71 recession, RCA and GE exited the mainframe market: "IBM and the **BUNCH**" (Burroughs, UNIVAC, NCR, Control Data, Honeywell).

**Exact CK phrases:** "IBM and the seven dwarfs" / "IBM and the BUNCH" · the 1401 as "the Model T of computing"

**Most likely question:** How did IBM maintain dominance and what strategies did the seven dwarfs use? Answer: 1401 mass-market success + System/360 architectural bet + three survival strategies (compatible/own family/niche).

### Ch 7 — Real Time Computing (1944–1973) 🔴

**Story arc:** Real-time (continuous interaction with ongoing events, as opposed to batch processing) emerged from military necessity and spread to civilian life through **entrepreneurial appropriation** — the chapter's central thesis. CK calls this a "blending of military research and entrepreneurial efforts."

**Project Whirlwind** (MIT, **Jay Forrester**, started 1944) was initially an aircraft simulator for the Navy. **Perry Crawford** "saved Whirlwind twice." First save: in 1945 Crawford convinced Forrester to go *digital* rather than analogue — without this Whirlwind would have been obsolete. Second save: when the Navy's interest cooled and funding was about to be cut around 1949–50, Crawford redirected Whirlwind toward air defence, making it the prototype for SAGE. **Bill Papian** introduced **magnetic core memory** (1951), solving the storage bottleneck.

**SAGE** (Semi-Automatic Ground Environment, operational 1958) — 23 direction centres, each running an **IBM AN/FSQ-7**, the largest computer ever built. CK's irony: SAGE was already militarily obsolete on delivery because ICBMs (not bombers) were now the threat. **It was not offering the safety it was supposed to create.** But SAGE created: CRT culture (early displays), reliable core memory, light-pen interaction, and trained an entire generation of computer engineers.

**Civilian appropriation:** IBM took SAGE expertise into **SABRE** (1957–1964, with American Airlines) — 1,100 terminals in 50 cities for real-time reservations. SABRE became the template for **credit-card networks** (Diners Club 1949 cards → BankAmericard 1958 → Visa 1970), **ATMs** (Barclays 1967), and **UPC** barcodes (1973, a McKinsey + IBM + grocery industry political coalition).

**Exact CK phrases:** "blending of military research and entrepreneurial efforts" · Whirlwind "saved twice" · SAGE was "obsolete on delivery" / "not offering the safety it was supposed to create"

**Most likely questions:**
- "Perry Crawford saved Whirlwind twice — explain" → name both saves (digital decision + redirect to air defence).
- "Real time as military experiment that almost didn't happen and didn't deliver safety" → Whirlwind near-cancellation, ICBM obsolescence, but civilian payoff via SABRE/credit/ATMs.

### Ch 8–9 — New Ways of Computing / Timesharing & Personal Computing roots (1961–1977) 🔴

**Story arc:** Computing was institutional, batch, expensive. **Timesharing** (multiple users on terminals sharing one mainframe) re-imagined the computer as personal, interactive, accessible to ordinary people. CK frames this as "new ways of computing" that made later personal computing thinkable.

**CTSS** (Compatible Time-Sharing System, **MIT 1961**, Fernando Corbató) — first practical timesharing. **Project MAC** (MIT, 1963) commercialised the concept. **GE** chose timesharing as its niche (from Ch 6 strategy).

**BASIC** (**Dartmouth 1964**, John Kemeny & Thomas Kurtz) — explicitly designed for non-specialist undergraduates. Combined with timesharing this meant a student with a terminal could *write programs* — a previously unimaginable thing. This is the "ordinary people" claim.

**"The computer utility"** — the dream (J.C.R. Licklider, John McCarthy) that computing would become like electricity: dial it up, pay by the second. **MULTICS** was the most ambitious attempt; commercially it failed, but its ideas seeded **UNIX** (Thompson & Ritchie at Bell Labs, 1969).

**Whole Earth Catalogue** (**Stewart Brand**, 1968) is the chapter's cultural pivot. Brand's slogan: **"access to tools."** The Catalogue listed everything from geodesic dome plans to early computer terminals as tools of liberation. Brand bridged Bay Area counterculture and computer engineers — the "two cultures" of California. The 1972 *Rolling Stone* article "Spacewar: Fanatic Life and Symbolic Death Among the Computer Bums" extended this. By the time the Altair came out (1975) the cultural ground was prepared.

**Exact CK phrases:** "new ways of computing" · "the computer utility" · "access to tools" · BASIC made computing "interesting for ordinary people"

**Most likely questions:**
- "Timesharing essential for computing being interesting for ordinary people — explain" → CTSS technical breakthrough + BASIC making programming approachable + terminal access changing who could use a computer.
- "Whole Earth Catalogue's influence on PC" → Brand bridges counterculture/engineering, "access to tools" reframes computing as liberation, prepares the cultural reception for the Altair.
- "Two new ways of computing in detail" → pick any two of: timesharing/CTSS, BASIC, the computer utility, the Whole Earth Catalogue's reframing.

### Ch 11 — The Internet (1969–2000) 🟡

**Story arc:** CK frames the Internet's rise as a **"confluence of three desires"**:

1. **Military desire (survivability):** **ARPANET** (1969), packet switching (Paul Baran at RAND, mid-60s), networks that survive partial destruction. Funded by DARPA. Key sites: UCLA, Stanford, UC Santa Barbara, Utah.
2. **Academic desire (information sharing):** Email turned out to be the killer app — by mid-1970s 75% of ARPANET traffic was email. Academics shared papers, debated ideas, formed online communities. Sociologists and psychologists (Sara Kiesler, Lee Sproull, Sherry Turkle) became fascinated by the **new social issues** email created: flaming, status-flattening, identity play, group polarisation.
3. **Commercial desire (mass communication and commerce):** **TCP/IP** (Vint Cerf, Bob Kahn, 1974) standardised inter-network communication. **NSFNET** (1986) connected universities. The **World Wide Web** (**Tim Berners-Lee**, **CERN**, 1989) added human-readable hypertext. **Mosaic browser** (1993, Marc Andreessen) → **Netscape** (1994) commercialised it. The 1995 NSFNET privatisation handed the network to private telcos. Dot-com boom and bust (1995–2001).

The three desires don't always align. Academic openness vs. commercial control. Military survivability vs. commercial reliability. These tensions persist (net neutrality, content moderation, surveillance).

**Exact CK phrases:** "confluence of three desires" · "packet switching" · "killer app" (email)

**Most likely question:** "Confluence of three desires" → name all three, give an example for each, explain one tension.

### Ch 14 — Search, Advertising, Surveillance Capitalism (1998–2020s) 🟡

**Story arc:** Early search engines (AltaVista, Yahoo, Excite, Lycos) used keyword or directory methods — none scaled well. **Google** (Page & Brin, Stanford PhD project, founded **September 1998**) brought **PageRank** (use the link graph as a relevance signal — pages linked by many other good pages are themselves likely good). The revenue problem: search is "free" — how to monetise? **AdWords** (2000), refined into **AdSense** (2003): auction-based ads keyed to search queries and (crucially) user data. The genius: ads got more valuable the more Google knew about the user.

### Ch 15 — Computing and Governance (2010s–2020s) 🟡

**Story arc:** 21st-century computing produced **ethical liabilities** that nation-states had to address:

1. **Privacy / data protection:** EU's **GDPR** (2018) — data as a fundamental right, explicit consent, right to erasure, fines up to 4% of global turnover. Versus US: sectoral approach (HIPAA for health, COPPA for kids' data), reliance on FTC enforcement, no general data right. **California Consumer Privacy Act (CCPA, 2018)** is partial US convergence.
2. **Platform liability:** US **Section 230** (1996) — platforms not liable for user content, enabling Web 2.0. EU **Digital Services Act / Digital Markets Act** (2022) — large platforms have duties, transparency, content moderation responsibilities.
3. **Algorithmic accountability:** AI bias in hiring, lending, criminal sentencing (COMPAS). EU **AI Act** (2024) — risk-based regulation of AI systems. US: sectoral, slow.
4. **Antitrust / competition:** US tradition (Sherman Act, Clayton Act) — applied late to tech (Microsoft 1998). EU **DMA** more aggressive against "gatekeepers".

**Underlying difference in legal philosophy:** US treats data and speech as essentially commercial / First-Amendment-protected; EU treats data and dignity as fundamental rights derived from post-WWII human-rights tradition.

### Model B10 — "Broadening the appeal" (the question you lost points on)

> *According to Campbell-Kelly, the personal computer industry in the 1980s "broadened the appeal" of computers in several ways. Explain that remark. Mention the main firms.*

**Intro (thesis):** Campbell-Kelly uses "broadened the appeal" to describe how, during the 1980s, the personal computer ceased to be a hobbyist or specialist machine and became accessible to ordinary households, small businesses, and non-programmers. Three mechanisms drove this broadening: the graphical user interface, productivity software, and the IBM-compatible open architecture.

**Body 1 — GUI (Apple, Microsoft, with credit to Xerox PARC):** Until the early 1980s using a computer meant typing commands. The graphical user interface — windows, icons, menus, pointer — originated at **Xerox PARC** (Smalltalk, Alto) but reached the mass market through Apple's **Macintosh** (1984), marketed as "the computer for the rest of us." Microsoft followed with **Windows**, becoming usable with version 3.0 in 1990. The GUI broadened the appeal by removing the requirement that users learn a command-line language — anyone could point and click.

**Body 2 — Software that made ownership worthwhile (VisiCorp, Lotus, Microsoft):** Hardware alone was not enough; people had to have a reason to buy. Word processors (WordPerfect, WordStar) and later **Microsoft Office** made the PC indispensable for office work. Crucially this was *not* games — the early 1980s saw a games industry crash, so productivity software did the broadening.

**Body 3 — Open architecture and clones (IBM, Compaq):** When IBM launched the **PC in 1981** with an open architecture and off-the-shelf parts, it inadvertently created a clone industry led by **Compaq** (1982). Hardware competition drove prices down sharply through the 1980s, putting computers within reach of small businesses and middle-class households.

**Conclusion:** Campbell-Kelly's claim is that the 1980s broadening was less about raw computing power than about *accessibility* — design (GUI), purpose (productivity software), and price (open architecture) had to converge. The firms that drove the shift — Apple, Microsoft, IBM, Lotus, Compaq — each contributed one piece.

### Model B7 — Real time computing / Whirlwind

**Intro:** Campbell-Kelly's chapter on real-time computing makes two claims that initially seem to undermine the technology's importance: Project Whirlwind nearly never produced a real-time computer at all, and once SAGE became operational it was strategically obsolete. ### Model C6 — AI winters vs. Babbage/Aiken, through agendas

> *Compare the narrative of the AI winters to the narrative of Babbage that Aiken used to obtain funding at Harvard. Use the concept of agendas.*

**Intro:** Both the Babbage-Aiken narrative and the AI-winter narrative are examples of how histories are told in the service of present agendas. Howard Aiken used Babbage's incomplete Analytical Engine to legitimise his own machine at Harvard in the 1940s; Pamela McCorduck and others have used the "AI winter / AI spring" cycle to legitimise continued AI funding. In both cases the historical claim is selective, and the agenda explains the selection.

**A — Babbage as precursor:** Charles Babbage (1791–1871) designed but never completed the Difference Engine and Analytical Engine. By the 1930s he was a half-forgotten curiosity. **Howard Aiken**, seeking IBM and Harvard support for the **Mark I** (1937–1944), constructed a narrative in which he was the engineer finally completing Babbage's vision. The technical reality was different — the Mark I was an electromechanical calculator with little architectural similarity to the Analytical Engine. But the narrative worked: Babbage as ancestor gave Aiken's project a venerable lineage and a sense of historical inevitability. **The agenda was funding legitimation; the history served the agenda.**

**B — AI winters:** The "AI winter" framing — episodes of disappointed funding (post-Lighthill, 1973; expert-systems collapse, late 1980s) followed by renewal — is similarly purposive. **Pamela McCorduck** (*Machines Who Think*, 1979 and later editions) and AI advocates use it to argue (i) AI is a *resilient* field, (ii) past failures came from oversold expectations that have now been corrected, and (iii) the current round is "different this time." The narrative legitimises continued investment by historicising failure as cyclical rather than terminal.

**C — The relation:** Both narratives use the past to authorise the present. In Aiken's case the past is a precursor (continuity); in the AI-winter case the past is a warning that has been learned from (rupture). Both work because they make present claims sound grounded in a longer story. The concept of **agendas** does not say these narratives are false — Babbage *did* design proto-computers; AI funding *did* collapse and revive — but it asks: *who benefits from this telling?* In both cases the answer is the institution (Harvard's IBM-funded lab; modern AI labs seeking sustained investment).

**Conclusion:** The history of computing and AI is repeatedly mobilised in the service of funding and legitimacy. Recognising the agendas behind a historical narrative is not a debunking move — it is part of reading the history honestly.

## 9. THE 10.0 LAYER — PUSHING FROM 9 TO PERFECT

**Reality check:** 10s are rare in the Dutch system. Beckers himself wrote that "a gpa of 8 at a Dutch university is considered excellent." Aiming for 10.0 means aiming for clean 2.0s on all four questions with zero half-marks lost.

**The math:** 1.0 gratispunt + 8 (four 2.0 answers) + 1 bonus = 10.0. There is no margin. One 1.5 caps you at 9.5.

### The seven differentiators between 9.5 and 10.0

This prof is a **historian of science**. He grades not only what happened but *how the story is told*. The 10.0 layer is about making *thinking* visible, not just facts.

**1. Historiographical move in every answer.** State the fact, then add one sentence about why this is the dominant telling and who benefits. Example: not "ENIAC was built at the Moore School in 1945" but "ENIAC is conventionally placed at the origin of computing because the Moore School narrative served IBM-Harvard-NSF agendas in the post-war period — but as Stephanie Dick has argued, histories of computing encode cultural dispositions that exclude other origins."

**2. Quote the question back.** First paragraph of every answer should explicitly engage the question's wording. *"Campbell-Kelly's phrase 'broadened the appeal' is doing two distinct kinds of work here — technical accessibility on the one hand, cultural legitimacy on the other. This essay addresses both."* Signals you are thinking *with* the prof.

**3. One secondary author per answer.** Beyond CK, drop one of these names with a one-line argument:
- **Bolter** (*Turing's Man*, 1984) — defining technology
- **Zuboff** (*Surveillance Capitalism*, 2019) — behaviour as raw material
- **McCorduck** (*Machines Who Think*) — AI history as advocacy
- **Brooks** (*Mythical Man-Month*) — adding people to a late project makes it later
- **Stephanie Dick** — computing history encodes cultural dispositions
- **Jonnie Penn** ("Animo Nullius") — data as colonial *terra nullius*
- **Paul Edwards** (*The Closed World*) — Cold War shaped computer architecture
- **Medina** (*Cybernetic Revolutionaries*) — Project Cybersyn
- **Dijkstra** — structured programming as mathematical discipline

Even a parenthetical lift: *"(as Zuboff argues, the user is not the customer — the user is the product.)"*

**4. Take a clear position on "to what extent" questions.** Don't hedge with "somewhat" or "in some ways." Use the formula: *"To a* **large** *extent — for three reasons. However, one important qualification: …"* Beckers wrote in your feedback that "the purpose for the flashback here is unclear" — he wants you to *defend* your structural choices, not just make them. Always be explicit about what you are doing and why.

**5. A counterpoint that you then resolve.** A 9.5 answer is monotone — assertion, evidence, conclusion. A 10.0 answer has the move:

> *"One might object that the IBM PC's open architecture was an unintended consequence of haste rather than a deliberate broadening strategy. This is true — but the unintended-vs-deliberate distinction does not undo the effect. CK's claim is about outcomes for users, not intentions in Armonk."*

30 extra seconds, one extra paragraph, large grading payoff.

**6. A forward-pointing conclusion.** End every answer with an implication that opens further discussion — your topics.md prescribes this ("repeat thesis + follow-up question") but execute it cleanly every time. Examples:
- "This raises the question whether the same surveillance-capitalism dynamic will be replicated by foundation-model providers in the 2020s."
- "Whether LOGO-style constructivism has any future in the era of LLM tutors is an open question."
- "The unresolved tension between academic openness and commercial control persists in the current debate over open-weight AI."

**7. Mechanical perfectionism.** At the 10.0 margin these small things matter:
- **Spelling and grammar.** Rule (2) explicitly grades this. Pick US OR UK English and stay consistent (decide before the exam — "behaviour" vs "behavior", "organisation" vs "organization").
- **Word count discipline.** 250–280 words per answer. Under 200 looks thin. Over 350 looks padded.
- **Question labelling.** Rule (4): label each answer with the question number AND title.
- **Name + student number on every page.** Rule (1).
- **Visible structure.** Use paragraph topic sentences. Don't write five-sentence paragraphs. Each paragraph carries one argumentative beat.
- **Date accuracy.** Verify every date in your head before you write it. Wrong date = grader notices = loses trust.

### Two examples — what the 10.0 layer looks like in practice

**Example A — Same opening, two levels:**

> *9.5 version:* "The IBM PC, launched in 1981, had open architecture and helped broaden the appeal of computers."
>
> *10.0 version:* "Campbell-Kelly's claim that the IBM PC 'broadened the appeal' is partly accidental: IBM's August 1981 decision to use off-the-shelf parts and an open architecture was driven by speed-to-market, not by a broadening strategy. Yet the consequence — a clone industry led by Compaq from 1982 — broadened the appeal more decisively than any deliberate Apple-style design choice could have. This is the appropriation pattern in miniature: a technology's social effect is determined in use, not in design intent."

The 10.0 version names a firm (Compaq), a date (August 1981, 1982), introduces a complication (unintended vs. deliberate), and ties to a course concept (appropriation). Same length, dramatically richer.

**Example B — A model 10.0 conclusion paragraph:**

> "Three currents — military survivability, academic information-sharing, and commercial communication — converged to produce the Internet. Their persistence as separate desires explains why the current Internet remains contradictory: open enough for academic inquiry, militarised enough for state surveillance, commercial enough for surveillance capitalism. *Whether these three desires can remain in productive tension as AI-mediated content reshapes the medium is the open question of the 2020s.*"

Compare to a competent 1.5–2.0 conclusion: "These three desires together explain the rise of the Internet." Both are correct. Only one earns the 2.0.

### Time management for 10.0

In a 2h15 exam answering 4 questions:

| Phase | Time | What |
|---|---|---|
| Read all 26 questions | 5 min | Decide picks. Mark them with a small "✓". |
| Outline all 4 answers | 8 min (~2 min each) | Bullet thesis + 3 body beats + conclusion line. Outline ALL four before writing ANY. |
| Write 4 answers | 4 × 25 min = 100 min | One question fully (intro+body+conclusion) before moving to next. |
| Edit / sweep | 12 min (~3 min each) | Check chronology, spelling, exact-phrase use, question labels, name on each page. |
| Buffer | 10 min | For the answer that runs over. |

The outline-first approach is what separates 9.5 from 10.0. Most students go all-in on Q1 and run out of time on Q4. The last answer is where the 10 is lost. Outlining all four first guarantees the fourth answer is as developed as the first.

### The single biggest 10.0 lever

If you do only one thing differently: **add the historiographical sentence to every answer.** Beckers cares about agendas, prejudice, metaphors, and the construction of histories more than any other framing in the course. An answer that ends with "this telling serves [X] agenda" or "the story is constructed this way because [Y]" demonstrates exactly the analytical move the course is designed to teach. It is the single move most likely to push a 1.5 to a clean 2.0.
